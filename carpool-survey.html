This case study explains the supporting survey website created alongside the Tóggo app. While the app explores the interaction design of trusted shared journeys, this website focuses on the wider policy problem: how carpooling could be made legally clear, financially fair, environmentally useful, and worthwhile enough for people to consider.

Survey Website Rationale

Why a Custom Survey Website Was Needed

The subject of carpooling in Ireland is not just a product design problem. It involves public opinion, Revenue rules, transport law, insurance, environmental fairness, public transport policy, behavioural change, and impactful but realistic government incentives. A dedicated survey website allowed the research to present this context before asking users to respond.

A standard form tool such as Google Forms or SurveyMonkey could have collected responses, but it would not have presented the complexity of the problem in a clear or engaging way. This research needed a designed interface where users could read background information, compare policy options, review worked examples, understand the trade-offs, and then respond with more context. For that reason, the website is better understood as a case study with embedded survey points, rather than a conventional questionnaire. The aim was to reduce the number of isolated questions and encourage users to engage more deeply with the case content before giving their opinion.

01

Context Before Questions

Participants needed more than isolated survey questions. The site could explain the legal, financial, and policy context first, helping users understand why a simple “would you carpool?” question was not enough.

02

Custom Interaction Design

The survey could use layouts, comparison cards, custom sliders, counters, rate models, occupancy-based journey cost tables, incentive sections, and worked examples. These helped users understand how each price-per-kilometre model would change depending on the number of passengers, which would be difficult to explain clearly in a standard form builder.

03

Research as a Case Study

The website made the research longer-lasting and easier to present. The explanation, survey logic, proposed policy models, and findings could all be displayed as a designed public-facing artefact rather than hidden inside a form response spreadsheet.

Core purpose

The survey website was not a traditional data-collection form. It was designed as a public-facing research artefact that explains the legal and financial problem, introduces possible solutions, and asks users to make informed choices around cost-sharing, incentives, and the public value of getting cars off the road.

Survey Logic

What the Survey Asked Users to Consider

The website moved participants through a sequence of policy questions. Each section introduced a real barrier to carpooling in Ireland, then asked the user to consider what would feel fair, legal, realistic, and worthwhile.

Question Area 01

Who is affected?

The survey first defined different cost-sharing stakeholders: drivers, car-owning passengers, car-contributing passengers, and general passengers.

This question helped users think beyond a simple driver/passenger split and consider the different financial positions people bring into a shared journey.

Question Area 02

What costs are shared?

Users were shown the difference between variable journey costs and full ownership costs, including fuel, wear, depreciation, motor tax, and insurance.

This was important because a fair rate depends on whether passengers are contributing only to the journey itself or to the wider cost of keeping a car on the road.

Question Area 03

What rate is fair?

The survey compared three possible rate approaches based on Irish cost data, Revenue civil service mileage rates, and the legal need to avoid commercial hire or reward.

This asked users to judge the trade-off between affordability, driver participation, legal defensibility, and the risk of making carpooling look like paid transport.

Question Area 04

What would change behaviour?

Users were asked to think like policy makers and allocate a limited annual budget across realistic carpooling incentives.

This helped test which supports felt meaningful enough to change behaviour, beyond the existing benefits of saving money, fuel, and emissions.

Why this structure mattered

The goal was not to ask people a simple “would you carpool?” question. The survey challenged users to think about the market conditions that would make carpooling work: how to attract enough drivers, enough passengers, enough trust, enough legal clarity, and enough incentive without turning ordinary shared travel into a commercial transport service.

Legal & Policy Context

Why Carpooling Needs Clearer Public Guidance

Irish policy allows informal private cost-sharing, but the boundary between a lawful contribution and commercial payment is not always clear to ordinary users. This uncertainty creates a trust problem before a journey even begins.

Policy Issue 01

SPSV / Hire or Reward

A driver can accept contributions toward running costs, but cannot operate for profit or reward. Once a payment looks like commercial passenger transport, the journey risks falling into SPSV territory and would require the appropriate licence.

Policy Issue 02

Revenue Ambiguity

Informal petrol money is common, but digital payments create a clearer record of money changing hands. A platform needs a defined tax-free cost-sharing rate so drivers, passengers, and operators know when a payment is reimbursement rather than taxable income.

Policy Issue 03

Insurance Uncertainty

Informal carpooling may be permitted where no profit is made, but drivers still need confidence that regular shared travel, digital payments, and platform records will not create uncertainty with their insurer.

Policy problem

The legal framework is intended to protect passengers and regulated taxi services, but public uncertainty can discourage ordinary non-profit carpooling. This website therefore argues for clearer public guidance around cost-sharing, insurance, safeguarding, tax-free mileage, and the difference between shared travel and hire or reward.

Cost-Sharing Stakeholders

Why Stakeholder Balance Matters

Before considering any shared journey rate, the survey asked users to think about who is affected. People sharing a journey do not all have the same relationship to car ownership, running costs, insurance, tax, depreciation, or public transport alternatives.

The central challenge is balance. If the rate is too driver-led, everyone may want to drive because the contribution feels more attractive than being a passenger. If the rate is too passenger-led, drivers may feel they are carrying the vehicle, legal, and financial burden without enough recognition. A successful pool needs people to move between roles depending on the journey, not become fixed into one side of the market.

The pool problem

The environmental aim is to remove unnecessary cars from the road. The economic aim is to help people save money. To do both, the system has to strike a fair balance between people who have already invested in cars, tax, insurance, NCT, maintenance, and depreciation, and people who have no vehicle stake and need an affordable travel option. The rate must make driving worth offering, but not so rewarding that the passenger side of the pool collapses.

Stakeholder 01

Car-Owning Driver

Legally owns and insures the car. Pays all ownership costs: insurance, motor tax, NCT, servicing, depreciation, and repairs, whether or not any journey takes place.

Provides the vehicle, organises the trip, bears the legal responsibility, and does the driving. This person carries the highest operational and financial burden.

“The fuel is paid for — I might as well take on extra passengers to recoup my costs.”

Stakeholder 02

Car-Owning Passenger

Owns a car but chooses to leave it at home for this journey. Still pays ownership costs on their parked car, but saves fuel, mileage-based wear, and some depreciation by not driving.

This stakeholder is crucial because they represent the strongest opportunity to reduce single-occupancy trips. They have a car, but may choose not to use it if the system feels fair.

“My car is sitting at home depreciating — I need a good reason to be the passenger.”

Stakeholder 03

Car-Contributing Passenger

Does not legally own the car, but may have a financial stake in a household vehicle: contributing to insurance, tax, fuel, repairs, or the purchase of the car.

This person may already contribute to car costs, so a policy model that treats them exactly like a general passenger may feel unfair. They may also be a learner driver or named driver who cannot offer driving in this system.

“I already contribute to running a car, so I wouldn’t want to pay as if I have no stake at all.”

Stakeholder 04

General Passenger

Has no connection to a vehicle. Relies on public transport, lifts, walking, cycling, or informal help from others. This is the most price-sensitive group.

This stakeholder is especially important in rural Ireland, where public transport may not provide a realistic alternative for many everyday journeys.

“It may be convenient, but is it better value than public transport or another lift?”
Cost Per Kilometre

Developing a Fair Tax-Free Mileage Rate

A tax-free rate per kilometre would provide the clearest and most transparent basis for digital carpool cost-sharing. It gives drivers and passengers a government-verifiable figure that can be shown before the journey begins, helping to frame the payment as reimbursement for shared travel costs rather than income or reward.

The research therefore explored how a digital carpooling platform could define a fair reimbursement rate without making ordinary shared travel look like a commercial transport service. The challenge is not only deciding how much a passenger should pay, but deciding what that payment is actually contributing toward.

In the event that Ireland introduced a defined tax-free carpooling or reimbursement rate, the policy would need to distinguish between costs caused by a specific journey and the broader cost of owning a car. This distinction is especially important when considering depreciation, because not all vehicle value loss is caused by one individual trip.

What the user is being asked to consider

Before choosing a preferred cost-sharing model, users needed to consider three connected questions: what costs are directly caused by the journey, what wider ownership costs the driver already carries, and whether a future tax-free rate should be simple and universal or more specific to vehicle type and use.

01
Should passengers only share journey costs?

This focuses on the cost created by the trip itself, such as fuel, energy, wear, and mileage-related maintenance.

02
Should ownership costs be recognised?

This considers the fact that the driver supplies the vehicle and already carries costs such as insurance, tax, NCT, servicing, and depreciation.

03
How should depreciation be treated?

This asks whether a shared journey rate should separate depreciation caused by mileage from depreciation that happens simply because the car ages over time.

Cost Model 01

Variable Journey Cost

Variable journey cost looks only at the extra cost caused by the shared trip. This includes fuel or energy, tyre wear, oil, routine maintenance, and mileage-related wear. These are costs that exist because the journey takes place.

This model is closest to the idea of non-commercial reimbursement because the driver is not being paid for ownership, labour, or profit. The passenger is only contributing toward the actual use of the vehicle for that journey.

Considered, but harder to include

Parking and tolls may still matter in a real journey, but they are more situational. They depend on the route, destination, time of day, and whether the driver would have paid them anyway. For that reason, this model focuses mainly on energy and vehicle-use costs.

Cost Model 02

Recognised Full Motoring Cost

Recognised full motoring cost takes a wider view. It accepts that a car journey is not only fuel. The driver supplies the vehicle, carries responsibility, and pays ongoing ownership costs whether or not a specific passenger journey takes place.

This model considers costs such as insurance, motor tax, NCT, servicing, repairs, depreciation, and the general cost of keeping a vehicle road legal. It is stronger from the driver’s perspective, but it also raises more questions about where reimbursement ends and reward begins.

Policy consideration

A fuller motoring rate may be easier to justify where it is linked to an existing recognised mileage benchmark, but a carpooling platform would still need clear public guidance so the payment is understood as cost-sharing rather than income.

Depreciation Problem

Separating time-based depreciation from mileage-based depreciation

Depreciation is one of the hardest costs to include fairly because there is no single figure that applies cleanly to every vehicle. Depreciation varies depending on age, mileage, condition, make, model, fuel type, demand in the used-car market, and how the vehicle has been maintained. Newer vehicles usually lose value much more quickly, with a significant drop often happening as soon as the car leaves the dealership. By contrast, once a car is several years old, and especially around eight years or more, further value loss can become much less dramatic. Because of this variable nature, there is no simple public data point that can perfectly separate how much value is lost through time and how much is lost through one additional journey.

However, if a tax-free carpooling reimbursement rate were created, this distinction would still need to be considered to balence the pool of drivers and whether somone drives or travels as passenger and leaves their car at home. Mileage-based depreciation is more directly connected to a shared trip because the journey adds kilometres to the car. Time-based depreciation is different: it happens even when the car remains parked. Separating the two makes the rate more honest about what a passenger is actually being asked to share, but it also affects how the carpooling market behaves. If the rate over-recognises fixed ownership costs, car-owning passengers may be less willing to leave their own car at home, because they may feel they should drive instead to recoup costs they have already paid, such as insurance, tax, and depreciation. This might create a tension between car-owning drivers and car-owning passengers, because both may want to be the driver rather than leave a depreciating asset sitting in the driveway.

Policy Models

Three Tax-Free Reimbursement Models

The three models below are proposed as mileage-only reimbursement approaches for non-commercial carpooling. They are not intended to cover tolls, parking, booking fees, waiting time, or platform charges. Those costs would need separate rules. The aim is to define how much a driver could receive per kilometre as tax-free reimbursement, rather than as taxable income, reward, or a commercial fare.

The difference between the models is not whether the driver earns money. All three are framed around reimbursement. The difference is how that reimbursement is calculated: whether it should be limited to the direct cost of the journey, based on a recognised full motoring-cost benchmark, or calculated through an occupancy-based formula that splits the journey cost while recognising the driver’s responsibility.

In this section, occupants means everyone in the car: the driver plus all passengers. The passenger rate is the amount each passenger would pay per kilometre.

A

Model A

Basic Variable Journey Cost

15c per km ceiling

A conservative mileage-only ceiling based on the direct variable costs caused by the journey.

This model sets a maximum reimbursement of approximately 15c per kilometre. It covers the costs created by making the journey, such as fuel or energy, oil and fluids, routine maintenance, tyre wear, and mileage-related wear and tear.

Clearest and easiest to defend as cost-sharing, but it does not recognise wider ownership costs such as insurance, motor tax, NCT, financing, or time-based depreciation.

B

Model B

Full Motoring Cost

31.79c per km ceiling

A fuller reimbursement ceiling based on Revenue’s civil service mileage benchmark.

This model uses Revenue’s Band 3 rate for engine capacity 1201cc–1500cc as the reference point. Revenue states that mileage claims for fully electric vehicles should use the 1201cc–1500cc engine-capacity category, making this band a more defensible and environmentally aligned reference than the higher large-engine rate. Revenue civil service rates reference

Strongest as an administrative benchmark because it relies on an existing Revenue mileage rate, but it may be less fair to passengers if they are asked to share the full cost of vehicle ownership.

C

Model C

Occupancy-Based Formula

15c ÷ occupants + 4.5c per passenger

A passenger-rate formula that splits the variable journey cost by occupancy, then adds a small driver-recognition amount.

This model separates the reimbursement into two parts. First, the 15c variable journey cost is divided between everyone in the car. Secondly, each passenger pays 4.5c per kilometre to recognise the driver’s responsibility, vehicle risk, and role in making the carpool possible.

Strongest for fairness and market balance, because passengers only pay for their seat while drivers are rewarded for fuller occupancy. However, it would be harder to legislate, verify, and police than a single fixed mileage ceiling.

Legal Defensibility

How Defensible Is Each Model?

All three models are proposed as tax-free mileage reimbursement approaches, not income-generating fares. The legal question is not whether the driver should earn money, but whether each model can be clearly recognised as reimbursement for motoring costs rather than taxable income, profit, or hire-or-reward transport.

Two separate frameworks would need to be satisfied. Revenue would need to recognise the payment as tax-free cost-sharing up to a defined mileage ceiling, while transport law would need to distinguish non-commercial carpooling from SPSV activity, hire or reward, or an app-based passenger fare.

A

Revenue — Strong

Model A is the most conservative tax position because it only recognises the direct variable cost of the journey. The driver cannot recover more than the proposed 15c/km mileage ceiling, so the payment is easiest to frame as cost-sharing rather than income.

Transport / NTA — Strongest

This is the clearest model against hire-or-reward characterisation because it does not include a driver-recognition amount or a wider ownership-cost payment. However, the 15c/km figure would still need to be formally defined.

B

Revenue — Strongest

Model B is strongest from an administrative Revenue perspective because it uses an existing civil service mileage-rate framework as its reference point. It therefore requires less new conceptual work than creating a completely new reimbursement method.

Transport / NTA — Moderate to Strong

It is defensible if clearly capped as reimbursement, but it is not automatically the safest transport-law model because it recognises fuller ownership costs rather than only the costs caused by the journey itself. It would need clear wording to avoid being interpreted as paid passenger transport.

C

Revenue — Moderate

Model C is more complex because it separates the variable journey cost from a fixed driver-recognition amount. That recognition amount would need to be clearly protected as part of a public reimbursement formula, rather than treated as profit or taxable reward.

Transport / NTA — Moderate

This model is stronger for fairness and occupancy, but harder to police. Because the driver receives more as more passengers join, legislation would need to make clear that the payment remains capped, non-commercial, and linked to shared travel rather than seat-selling.

Required change

Before any platform-facilitated passenger payment system could be legally safe, Revenue would need to define carpooling mileage receipts as tax-free reimbursement up to an agreed ceiling, and the Department of Transport or NTA would need to distinguish non-commercial carpooling from hire or reward. Until both steps are clarified, digital carpooling payments remain uncertain for drivers, passengers, insurers, and platforms.

Pool Points System

A Public-Interest Incentive Without Creating a Fare

Pool Points connect the policy survey back to the Tóggo app by rewarding verified shared travel separately from passenger payments. The points system is not part of the mileage reimbursement rate and is not paid by passengers to drivers. It is a capped public-interest incentive designed to encourage repeated shared travel without turning carpooling into a fare-based transport service.

The proposed annual cap is €150 per person. This is not presented as an existing Irish carpooling allowance; it is a realistic baseline proposal informed by comparable transport and tax-incentive structures, including Cycle to Work, TaxSaver, Small Benefit Exemption, and European sustainable mobility schemes.

€150
Annual public cap

Maximum monetary value per person per year. The cap keeps the incentive meaningful enough to encourage behaviour change, while preventing points from becoming a disguised fare or income stream.

€12.50
Monthly cap

The €150 annual cap is spread across 12 months. This creates a monthly ceiling of €12.50, encouraging consistent shared travel rather than allowing a user to claim too much in one unusually active month.

40
Monthly journey target

The full monthly target is based on 40 verified one-way shared journeys: 20 return commutes across a typical four-week working month.

Policy Benchmark

Why €150 is a realistic annual cap

The €150 cap is deliberately modest. It is large enough to feel useful when combined with cost-sharing and occasional raffle incentives, but small enough to remain clearly separate from wages, income, or a fare. It also mirrors the logic of existing Irish schemes that use capped, tax-efficient incentives to support greener commuting choices.

01

Cycle to Work comparison

Revenue’s Cycle to Work Scheme supports commuting by allowing bicycles and safety equipment to be provided tax-free up to set limits. A standard bicycle limit of €1,250, available once every four years, gives a useful comparison for an annualised green-commuting incentive. Revenue Cycle to Work reference

02

TaxSaver comparison

TaxSaver already supports regular commuting through a salary-sacrifice style structure, where employees can save tax, USC, and PRSI on public-transport tickets. A carpooling incentive could follow the same public-interest logic: reward greener commuting without treating it as ordinary income. TaxSaver reference

03

Voucher-style delivery

Revenue’s Small Benefit Exemption shows that Ireland already uses capped non-cash benefits through tax rules. A Pool Points reward could be delivered as a restricted transport, fuel, grocery, or mobility voucher rather than cash. Small Benefit Exemption reference

Monthly Cap Logic

Reward repeated shared behaviour, not one-off journeys

The monthly cap is designed around commuter behaviour. One return commute equals two one-way shared journeys. A full monthly target of 40 one-way journeys represents 20 return commutes across four working weeks. The system therefore rewards repeated verified behaviour rather than occasional novelty use.

Monthly cap formula

40 verified one-way journeys × 30 max points = 1,200 points, plus a 50-point target bonus when the monthly journey target is reached. This gives a maximum of 1,250 points per month. At 100 points = €1, the monthly value is capped at €12.50.

10

Starter journeys

Early use of the system. At an example average of 15 points per journey, 10 verified shared journeys would return 150 points, or €1.50 in monthly value.

20

Regular journeys

Around 10 return shared commutes. At an example average of 18 points per journey, 20 verified shared journeys would return 360 points, or €3.60 in monthly value.

30

Strong participation

A strong monthly habit. At an example average of 22 points per journey, 30 verified shared journeys would return 660 points, or €6.60, and could unlock raffle eligibility.

40

Full monthly target

The full commuter benchmark. At the maximum journey score, 40 journeys produce 1,200 points, plus the 50-point target bonus, giving the full €12.50 monthly cap.

Points Per Journey

Maximum 30 points per verified shared trip

A verified shared trip receives base points, then earns additional points for occupancy, distance, and relay reliability. Most journeys would not reach the full 30-point maximum. The aim is to spread rewards across several behaviours: verified completion, fuller cars, reasonable journey distance, and reliable confirmation.

+10

Verified shared trip

Base points for a completed shared journey with two or more verified users. Solo journeys earn no points because the public value comes from removing unnecessary single-occupancy car use.

+10

Occupancy weighting

Fuller cars earn more because occupancy creates the clearest public benefit. A fuller vehicle can remove more cars from the road, reduce duplicated fuel use, and make better use of an existing journey.

+5

Distance bonus

Distance is rewarded because longer shared journeys can save more fuel and emissions. However, it is capped so that distance does not become more important than occupancy or allow long trips to dominate the reward system.

+5

Relay reliability

Reliability points reward users who confirm, authenticate, and complete the journey properly. This supports trust and reduces cancellations, ghost bookings, or unverified reward claims.

Occupancy Weighting

Fuller cars earn more — for everyone

Occupancy is weighted more strongly than distance because the core aim is not to reward people for travelling further. The aim is to make existing journeys more efficient by filling empty seats. A short journey with a full car can create stronger public value than a longer journey with only one passenger.

Realistic Monthly Examples

Most users would earn below the full cap

The cap shows the maximum possible value, not the expected value for every user. In practice, many journeys would score below the 30-point maximum because they may not be full occupancy, long distance, or fully relay-bonus eligible.

Why the reward is still useful

Pool Points are intentionally small as a direct monetary reward. Their purpose is not to replace the main cost-sharing benefit of carpooling. Instead, they add a visible public-interest reward on top of passenger cost-sharing, fuel savings, lower individual travel costs, and possible raffle prizes. The combined effect makes repeated carpooling feel more worthwhile without turning the system into a fare.

System safeguards
  • No solo journey points: a shared trip must include two or more verified users.
  • Points are separate from cost-sharing and passenger payments.
  • Rewards are capped monthly and annually to prevent overpayment.
  • Route match, GPS activation, confirmation stages, and duplicate-account review protect the reward system.
  • Maximum reward-eligible trips per day should prevent abuse.
  • Human exceptions are needed so emergency cancellations do not unfairly punish users.
Incentives Funnel

How Can We Incentivise Carpooling?

The incentives section asked users to think like government decision-makers. If a limited annual budget existed for each carpooler, where should that money go: direct payments, tax relief, platform subsidies, public transport integration, or infrastructure?

The purpose of the funnel was to separate what sounds attractive from what is realistic in Irish policy conditions. Some ideas are powerful but slow, expensive, or dependent on infrastructure. Others are less dramatic, but could move sooner.

Most plausible
Direct financial and voucher supports

Cash onboarding bonuses and Pool Points vouchers can be funded without new roads, NTA integration, or major infrastructure.

Policy pipeline
Tax and public-system supports

Motor tax discounts, tax credits, TaxSaver-style wallets, and Leap integration may be useful, but require stronger administrative or legislative coordination.

Least plausible
Infrastructure-heavy incentives

HOV lanes and preferential parking can be powerful, but they involve enforcement, council revenue, road capacity, planning, and long delivery timelines.

Ranked incentives

From most ready to hardest to implement

These incentives were planned for the survey website so users could choose between options that differ in speed, cost, fairness, target user, and political feasibility.

01

Cash Onboarding Bonus

A once-off payment for new carpoolers who complete a verified number of trips within a fixed time period. Strong for reducing first-use inertia.

02

Pool Points — Vouchers & Prize Draws

Verified trips earn points that can convert to retail vouchers or prize draw entries. Works for drivers and passengers equally and does not require new legislation.

03

Motor Tax Discount

Drivers use an app-generated annual journey certificate to claim a tiered reduction. Strong driver incentive, but requires county council administration.

04

Annual Income Tax Credit

Consistent carpoolers claim a tiered tax credit through Revenue myAccount. Strong precedent exists, but it depends on the Budget cycle and Finance Bill change.

05

TaxSaver Mobility Wallet

Salary-sacrifice mobility wallet funding could support carpool payments pre-tax, but it mainly helps people in employment and requires wider policy development.

06

Leap Card Integration

Carpooling could connect to national transport ticketing, but this depends on NTA cooperation and future account-based ticketing infrastructure.

07

Preferential or Free Parking

Priority spaces at public car parks, park-and-ride locations, or employer sites could reward verified carpooling, but council revenue and local adoption create barriers.

08

HOV Lanes

Dedicated lanes could reward carpooling with time savings, but require major infrastructure, enforcement cameras, road-capacity decisions, and long-term funding.

Survey incentive question

If the government allocated €100 per carpooler per year, how would you spend it? The question asked users to allocate a limited budget across the incentives that would most influence their decision to carpool. Allocating money to infrastructure meant prioritising public investment rather than direct personal payment.

Case Study Outcome

What the Survey Website Added to the Project

The survey website extended the Tóggo project beyond app design. It made the legal, policy, cost-sharing, and incentive problem visible, and created a public-facing artefact that could be shown to transport stakeholders, government departments, local authorities, and sustainability groups.

01

Public Understanding

It helped explain why carpooling is difficult to implement legally in Ireland without clearer cost-sharing rules and public guidance.

02

Policy Direction

It framed possible approaches for tax-free mileage rates, Revenue treatment, SPSV distinction, insurance confidence, and realistic public incentives.

03

Design Evidence

It supported the Tóggo app concept by showing that product design, law, trust, behaviour, and government policy need to work together.

Final outcome

The carpool survey website became a bridge between UX research and policy design. It showed that a carpooling platform cannot succeed through interface design alone: users need legal clarity, fair cost-sharing, realistic incentives, trusted verification, and a balanced pool where driving and being a passenger both feel worthwhile.
