edHub

Unified, accessibility-first lesson planning for primary education — AI-assisted and aligned to EAA.

Role: UX research, IA, UI/brand, prototyping

Duration: Concept → MVP roadmap

Overview & Problem

Teachers juggle multiple tools to plan lessons and differentiate for AEN learners. Accessibility is inconsistent, and with the European Accessibility Act, inclusive design becomes baseline. edHub centralises planning, resources, differentiation, and accessibility checks in one responsive workspace.

  • Time drain: scattered tools and rework.
  • Differentiation gap: limited support for learning profiles.
  • Accessibility: need built-in checks and supports (EAA-aligned).
Primary Research

Survey of the Target Market

The survey was completed by four working education professionals: three primary-school teachers and one curriculum coordinator. The group included both early-career and experienced educators, and the questions explored their current lesson-planning tools, support for children with different learning needs, accessibility, AI-assisted planning, online resources, and student assessment information.

Finding 01

Current tools save time, but do not cater well for different learning needs

All four respondents had used lesson-planning software. Time saving and pre-populated Department of Education curriculum content were common benefits, but all four also selected “not effective in differentiation” as a problem.

In this context, differentiation means adapting a lesson, activity, level of difficulty, or type of support so that children with different abilities, learning styles, or additional educational needs can work toward the same learning goals.

Design implication: edHub should retain the time-saving benefits of existing planning tools while making it easier for teachers to adapt content for individual learners and different classroom needs.

Finding 02

Accessibility support was inconsistent and awareness was mixed

When asked whether their current online resources or planning tools provided accessibility options, two respondents answered No, one answered Some of them, and one answered I don’t know.

When asked which accessibility options they used in the classroom, adjustable font sizes were selected most often (3 / 4), followed by changing button size (2 / 4). Screen readers, dark mode, high-contrast mode, and keyboard navigation were each selected by one respondent.

The responses do not suggest that accessibility is unimportant. Instead, they point to uneven awareness and use: accessibility was not consistently visible in the planning tools participants used, while different educators relied on different supports in practice.

Design implication: accessibility features should be built into edHub and made easy to find, rather than treated as specialist settings hidden away from the main planning experience.

Finding 03

Interest in tools for different learning abilities was unanimous

All four participants said they would try a new lesson-planning tool if it included features designed for children with different learning abilities. This reinforced the differentiation problem identified earlier in the survey.

Three of the four participants had also used AI-powered tools for lesson planning. One respondent specifically disliked that the AI tools they had used could not plan for specific students in their class.

The individual comment is supported by the wider response to the learning-needs question: all four participants were receptive to a tool that explicitly helped teachers plan for children with different abilities.

Design implication: AI in edHub should support curriculum-aligned planning while allowing teachers to adapt the output for individual learners, different ability levels, and AEN requirements.

Finding 04

Easy access to assessment data was valued more strongly than deeper integration

Question 8 asked participants to rate six possible lesson-planning features from 1 (most important) to 6 (least important). Integration with online resources received the strongest ratings, with all four participants rating it either 1 or 2.

Integration with student assessments received a more mixed response: two participants rated it 2 and two rated it 4. In this context, integration means linking assessment data directly with the planning system so that results, student profiles, or progress information can actively support lesson planning and differentiation.

However, Question 18 asked a simpler and more practical question: whether it would be beneficial to have easy access to student assessments within an online planning tool. Here, 4 / 4 participants selected “Very Valuable”. Access refers to being able to quickly view or retrieve assessment information while planning, without necessarily using that data to automatically influence the lesson plan.

Question 14 also showed that assessment information was already being stored digitally in several forms. Student profiles were available to 4 / 4 participants, while standardised-test results and report cards were available to 3 / 4.

Design implication: The research suggests a useful distinction between access and integration. Educators clearly valued having assessment information readily available while planning, while deeper integration with assessment data was a more mixed priority. For edHub, this suggests prioritising easy access to existing student records first, with more advanced assessment-driven planning and differentiation developed as a deeper feature.

Visual Design

Branding & Styles

edHub's visual identity was designed to balance professionalism with a friendly and accessible educational experience. The system combines a calm colour palette, clear typography, modular layouts, and restrained visual effects to keep information organised while giving the interface a distinctive and contemporary character.

Colour Palette

Blue was selected as the primary colour because of its association with calmness, focus, and trust within educational environments. A muted yellow adds warmth and optimism, while plum and neutral tones provide contrast and support hierarchy throughout the interface.

#0081A7 • #670643 • #F9D978 • #2D3748 • #FBFAF8

Typography

Poppins Bold and Regular were selected for their clean, friendly appearance and strong legibility. The geometric letterforms give the interface a contemporary feel while remaining clear at smaller sizes across navigation, forms, dashboard elements, and longer pieces of content.

Aa Bb Cc — Poppins Bold / Poppins Regular

Bento Grid Layout

A bento-style layout was used to organise the large amount of information available within edHub. Related tools and information can be grouped into modular areas while still allowing each component to remain visually distinct.

This approach supports dashboard-style interfaces particularly well, allowing teachers to scan lesson plans, resources, assessments, student information, and supporting tools without presenting everything as one continuous block of content.

Glassmorphism, Gradients & Interaction States

Glassmorphism and gradients were used selectively rather than across every interface element. Semi-transparent surfaces help create depth within the bento-style layout while maintaining a clear separation between foreground controls and surrounding content.

Selected, unselected, hover, and active states were also designed to give users clear visual feedback as they move through the interface. These states help communicate which options are interactive, currently active, or awaiting input.

Components

Reusable components were designed to fit within the bento-style interface, including glassmorphism in selected areas, modal pop-ups such as the AI assistant chatbox, selected and unselected states, and input states replicating HTML form validation.

Market Research

Market Positioning & Competitive Landscape

Competitor research was used to understand where existing lesson-planning platforms were already performing well and where opportunities remained for edHub. The analysis focused particularly on accessibility, support for different learning needs, AI-assisted planning, responsiveness, educational resources, and the Irish education context.

SWOT Analysis

Findings from the competitor research were brought together to identify the strengths and weaknesses of the existing market, along with opportunities for edHub and potential threats from established education platforms.

S
Strengths
  • Access to large databases of resources and content for lesson-planning.
  • Teacher-first, Ireland-specific platforms can provide value through alignment with the Irish curriculum and Irish-language resources.
  • AI tools are already being used to generate lesson plans on platforms such as Twinkl and TeachMateAI, demonstrating an established use case for AI-assisted planning.
W
Weaknesses
  • Accessibility features were limited or not clearly available across the platforms examined.
  • Existing platforms provided limited support for adapting lesson plans to children with different learning abilities or Additional Educational Needs (AEN).
  • Two of the competitor websites examined were not fully responsive across different screen sizes.
O
Opportunities
  • edHub could address gaps in the existing market by combining accessibility features with lesson-planning tools that allow teachers to adapt content for AEN students and different learning abilities.
  • Collaboration with trusted publishers, curriculum providers, and educational bodies could provide access to established resources while helping the platform build credibility.
  • Connecting curriculum resources, student information, assessments, and AI-assisted planning within one system could reduce the need for teachers to move between multiple tools.
T
Threats
  • TeachMateAI and Twinkl are large international platforms with established user bases and existing AI functionality.
  • Scoilnet has strong recognition within Irish education through its connection with the Department of Education.
  • Features introduced by edHub could potentially be replicated by larger competitors with greater development resources.

Validation

Moderated Usability Testing

The prototype was tested in person with a 52-year-old primary school principal who had more than 20 years' experience and had previously used several lesson-planning products. She was asked to think aloud while completing tasks so navigation issues, unclear labels, and friction points could be observed directly.

Goals
  • Test whether the overall edHub concept was understood.
  • Identify errors and broken or confusing interactions.
  • Reveal friction in navigation, labels, and task completion.
Usability factors
  • Effectiveness and efficiency in completing goals.
  • Legibility, content balance, and satisfaction.
  • Ability to recognise and recover from errors.
What worked
  • The participant completed 80% of the assigned tasks.
  • The overall product concept and purpose were understood.
  • The Lesson-Planning Centre generated the strongest positive response.
  • Pre-populated, curriculum-aligned content was appreciated.
  • The participant said she would be likely to subscribe.
What needed work
  • Some menu navigation was difficult and one link was broken.
  • The purpose of some tools was not immediately visible.
  • The label and purpose of the "Need help" function caused confusion.
  • The student statistics comparison was unsuitable for larger groups.

Changes Made After Testing

Feedback was converted into specific interface changes rather than being left as a list of observations.

Outcomes & Impact

  • Responsive, accessibility-led planning experience.
  • Differentiation at the point of planning (not afterthought).
  • Cleaner evidence tracking (notes, exports, light analytics).

Product Strategy

Getting edHub to Market

The report proposes a three-stage development plan: release a focused Minimum Viable Product, expand the platform as the user base grows, and then complete the advanced feature set for a broader launch. Each phase is tied to a development objective, testing activity, and cost range.

Concept. Develop. Deploy. Educate. Optimise. Scale. Repeat

1

Minimum Viable Product Launch

Phase 1 focuses on releasing a functional MVP with the essential edHub experience. The proposal combines the team's UX, development, marketing, and operations skills with strategic outsourcing for selected technical work.

  • AI development and Natural Language Processing.
  • Core full-stack product development.
  • Cloud infrastructure, quality assurance, and legal preparation.
  • First market release with the essential lesson-planning workflow.

Reported Phase 1 total: EUR 107,000-EUR 170,000

2

Further Development and Scale

Phase 2 develops the MVP into a more capable and scalable product. Existing functions are refined while new services are introduced to support a growing user base and more complex school workflows.

  • Further AI development and interface refinement.
  • OCR integration for extracting information from documents.
  • APIs for automated grading and language translation.
  • Cloud optimisation, expanded user testing, and quality assurance.

Reported Phase 2 total: EUR 208,000-EUR 352,000

3

Full Product Launch

The final phase prepares edHub for a wider market with a polished experience and a more complete feature set. Testing and feedback continue so the platform can support users across different markets.

  • Further AI development and deep-learning work.
  • Ask Edy, automated grading, and expanded OCR capability.
  • Final interface refinement and comprehensive QA.
  • Marketing, promotion, and preparation for broader rollout.

Reported Phase 3 total: EUR 378,600-EUR 551,600
