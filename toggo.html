Tóggo

A UX and interaction design case study exploring how a carpooling platform, supported by clearer legal and policy guidance, could make shared travel in Ireland feel safer, more transparent, more reliable, and more worth choosing.

Project type: MA Interaction Design

Role: UX research, interaction design, policy-informed concept, prototyping, brand system, testing

Overview

Reliability Relayed

Tóggo is a proposed government-aligned carpooling platform for Ireland. It focuses on the human side of carpooling: trust, accountability, journey commitment, clear confirmations, and realistic incentives that are strong enough to shift attitudes towards shared travel.

Design Framework

Double Diamond Research & Design Process

I used the Double Diamond because the project started with a broad and ambiguous problem rather than a fixed app idea. Carpooling in Ireland touches law, trust, safety, behaviour, public transport, cost, insurance, and social attitudes, so the early stages needed to open the problem up before narrowing it into a focused design response.

The framework helped separate the work into four useful phases: discovering the barriers, defining the core problem, developing possible directions, and delivering a prototype that could test how trust, reliability, and journey commitment might be designed into the experience.

Process Timeline

From Discovery to Prototype

The project moved through a structured design process, beginning with a broad investigation into why carpooling remains underdeveloped in Ireland, before narrowing into two connected outputs: the Tóggo app prototype and the supporting policy/survey website.

01

Discovery Phase

I began by examining why carpooling has not become a stronger transport option in Ireland, despite its social, environmental, and financial potential.

Desk research explored car dependency, rural and commuter-town access, cost-sharing rules, SPSV restrictions, insurance uncertainty, safety concerns, environmental impact, and existing guidance.

The literature review identified key barriers, including legal confusion, trust, safety, convenience, journey timing, access, and uncertainty around what users are allowed to do.

Surveys and interviews explored people’s attitudes, behaviours, and experiences of carpooling and shared travel. Other shared transport models were also reviewed to understand what needs they meet in Ireland and what Tóggo could learn from them.

Policy and transport discussions helped clarify what may be realistic, what would require wider change, and what sits outside the project scope.

02

Definition Phase

In this phase, I narrowed the project into a clearer problem area: how interaction design can make carpooling in Ireland feel safer, clearer, more trusted, and easier to participate in.

The research was structured around legal, behavioural, safety, access, environmental, and policy-related factors. These areas helped shape the research question and define the user and stakeholder needs.

The project also began to separate into two connected directions: the policy blockers that prevent large-scale, digitally assisted adoption, and the concurrent human needs for better safety, reliability, and organisation.

Stakeholder mapping, early user needs, research themes, and journey scenarios were used to identify the main design opportunities.

03

Development Phase

Using the findings from the research phase, I developed three possible design routes for the project.

  • A state-facilitated carpooling app
  • An interactive case study and survey website
  • A pilot scheme or regulatory sandbox

It became clear that the app and survey website were closely connected and dependent on each other. The app showed what a safer, more organised carpooling system could look like, while the website explained the wider policy context and tested public attitudes needed to support greater participation.

For this reason, I decided to develop both Route 1 and Route 2. The pilot scheme remained outside the project scope, but could become a future stage after further research and policy support.

04

Prototyping Phase

The prototyping phase focused on building both parts of the final design direction.

The Tóggo app prototype was developed in Figma to show how a state-supported carpooling system could work in practice. The prototype focuses on safety, verification, trust, journey organisation, reliability, cost sharing, and incentives.

The supporting website was developed as a policy case study and survey. Its role is to explain the research context, present the app concept, show the proposed policy changes, and ask users whether these improvements would make carpooling feel safer, clearer, and more realistic in Ireland.

Together, the app and website allow the project to do two things: show what a better carpooling system could look like, and test whether people would support or participate in it. To date, only the policy section of the website has been completed, and further iterations are needed for full launch.

Research Context

Understanding Why Carpooling Feels Difficult in Ireland

The idea came from experiencing digital carpooling in other EU countries, where organised shared travel felt more permitted, normalised, and supported. After coming home to Ireland, the limits of the transport system became much more obvious: many people are still dependent on private cars, public transport does not always reach rural or commuter-town routines, and the rising cost of fuel makes everyday travel more difficult.

The research began from a tension: carpooling could support more sustainable, affordable, and accessible travel, especially where public transport is limited, but participation remains low. The core barriers were not only technical. They were behavioural, legal, social, emotional, and organisational.

Trust & Personal Safety

Sharing a private car journey means entering a confined space with another person. The research showed that users need confidence around identity, vehicle condition, accountability, familiar networks, and what happens if something goes wrong.

Reliability & Routine

Carpooling only becomes useful if it fits real routines. School runs, shift work, caring responsibilities, return journeys, detours, late arrivals, and cancellation anxiety all affect whether people feel able to depend on others.

Law, Policy & Incentives

The project also had to consider how people interpret cost sharing, insurance, hire-or-reward restrictions, tax treatment, and incentives. This is why the app became a policy-enabled concept rather than a purely private route-matching tool.

Research Approach

How the Research Was Structured

The research was structured around two evidence sources: secondary research and primary research. Secondary research established the wider transport, legal, policy, behavioural, and environmental context. Primary research tested these issues through a public attitudes survey and interviews with people who had carpooling experience, transport knowledge, legal/policy awareness, or professional stakeholder perspectives.

These sources were then synthesised into the main design challenge: although law, policy, insurance, and cost-sharing matter, the Tóggo app needed to focus on the human conditions that make carpooling feel safe, reliable, organised, and worth choosing.

Evidence & Findings

What the Research Revealed

Instead of separating the findings into “survey results” and “interview results”, the evidence was grouped by the main themes that shaped the design. Each finding combines quantitative survey patterns, qualitative interview insight, and the design implication for Tóggo.

Finding 01

Familiarity Drives Willingness

Participants were much more willing to share journeys with people they already knew or could socially place. Willingness dropped sharply when the match was based only on route similarity.

Interview evidence supported this. A participant with experience using European carpooling apps described profile photos, reviews, verified accounts, and visibility of other passengers as features that could make an uncomfortable situation feel more comfortable.

Design implication: Tóggo needed to go beyond route matching by introducing identity, verification, profile visibility, reviews, passenger transparency, and social reassurance.

Finding 03

Carpooling Is Most Relevant Where Transport Is Weakest

Secondary research showed that car dependency, rural access gaps, commuter-town growth, and limited public transport all increase the potential value of carpooling. The survey supported this, with most participants agreeing that limited public transport makes carpooling more relevant.

Professional interviews added that rural transport is often social as well as logistical. In some contexts, transport is connected to isolation, community access, and everyday independence.

Design implication: Tóggo should not be designed like a generic city rideshare app. It needs recurring journeys, local familiarity, commuter-town logic, rural access, and practical scheduling.

Incentives Need to Be Practical

Incentives mattered, but the research showed that they needed to feel grounded in everyday travel pressure. Saving fuel costs, reducing traffic, convenience, and environmental benefit were more useful than vague rewards.

App Logic

Ten Rules of the Pool

Tóggo is built around posted journeys rather than one-off searching. This was an important interaction decision because carpooling is not a single, linear flow. A driver post, passenger request, one-way trip, same-day return, recurring commute, group journey, or authenticated live journey can all lead to different outcomes.

The rules below define how Tóggo handles those journey permutations. Instead of asking users to repeatedly search and leave when no match appears, users post a journey into the Match Pool. This keeps the trip active, creates a sense of urgency around matching, and gives the system more time to find compatible journeys in the background.

01

Posted Journey Model

Search is deliberately reduced because a failed search gives the user no reason to return. A posted journey remains active in the Match Pool, giving the system more opportunity to find a compatible driver or passenger.

02

Conditional Journey Logic

Each journey type behaves differently. One-way, return, recurring, driver-led, passenger-led, and group journeys all need different rules, permissions, timings, and confirmation states.

03

Match Momentum

Tóggo is designed to keep a potential match moving forward while reducing the uncertainty that can make carpooling feel risky. Journey posts, reminders, confirmations, and live status updates help users know whether a trip is still going ahead, who has confirmed, and when plans have changed.

01

Trip Planning & Posting

Post your journey into the Match Pool.

Tóggo is built around planned journeys rather than sporadic searching. Users post their trip and the system keeps looking for matches over time, even when no immediate match exists.

02

One-Off Trips

Choose driver or passenger — never both.

A one-off trip cannot be posted as both roles. This reduces decision fatigue, shortens negotiation, and helps the system match each side of a journey more clearly.

03

Same-Day Return

Return trips are booked as linked legs.

Same-day returns link the outbound journey with the return journey. This supports commuting, appointments, events, and social journeys where the important reassurance is knowing the user can also get home.

04

Recurring Trips

Recurring journeys follow the schedule owner.

Recurring journeys can be weekly, several days, weekdays, or custom. The creator controls whether the journey is public, private, or shared with trusted groups.

05

Parking

Parking sits outside formal cost sharing.

Parking costs vary too widely by location, duration, and driver choice. They are excluded from the formal calculation to avoid disagreement and keep shared pricing clearer.

06

Tolls

Tolls are split across the carpool group.

Tolls are route-based and predictable, so they are included where the journey uses one. The lower e-tag rate is always split regardless of the driver having one or not.

07

Verification

All users must be verified.

Verification is required before posting, booking, messaging, viewing exact locations, or joining a live journey. Drivers also provide licence and vehicle evidence.

08

Limited Preview

Unverified users browse a preview only.

Users who have not onboarded can preview possible journeys, but cannot post, book, message, view full profiles, or see exact journey details.

09

Authenticate Journey

Journey authentication confirms the trip started.

Member authentication, location confirmation, route data, pickup, departure, arrival, delay, and cancellation confirmations support timing accuracy, accountability, and Pool Points.

10

Driver Rules

The driver sets the journey conditions.

Drivers set pickup options, departure time, route flexibility, luggage space, and passenger limits before matching. Users can view these preferences before accepting, or choose another journey. Potential matches may still appear outside the driver’s original preferences, but any arrangement remains at the driver’s discretion.

Prototyping

From Paper Wireframes to System Decisions

Paper wireframes were used to test the early structure of Tóggo before moving into higher-fidelity screens. The focus was onboarding, verification and journey planning, because these flows carried the most risk around trust, safety and platform access.

What was tested

Early flows with high system risk

01

What users should verify before joining or driving.

02

How much of the platform unverified users should see.

03

How journey posting, matching and confirmation should work.

01

Vehicle responsibility

The prototype moved away from document uploads for tax, insurance and NCT status. Instead, drivers confirm that their vehicle is legal, insured and roadworthy. This avoids suggesting that Tóggo has verified information it cannot reliably check.

02

Verified access

Unverified users can preview the service, but cannot post, book, message, view exact locations, join live journeys or earn Pool Points. Full access is unlocked through verification.

03

Gender and safety

Gender was removed from the prototype. The issue raised important questions around inclusion, fairness and women-only safety features, but required dedicated research before being designed into the system.

04

Relay points integrity

The wireframes also raised the risk of false journey confirmations. GPS proximity between driver and passenger was identified as the most direct way to support the relay confirmation and Pool Points system.

Prototype outcome

Paper prototyping moved Tóggo from a general carpooling idea into a rule-based platform where verification, access, responsibility and journey confirmation became core system logic.

Interaction Logic

Reliability Confirmation Flows

Two sequence flows were created to map the reliability logic behind Tóggo. The system addresses a simple but critical problem: when journeys are planned days or weeks in advance, people forget, change plans, or drop out without telling anyone.

The design challenge

For carpooling, reliability is not a small detail. A passenger left waiting will not trust the platform again. A driver who holds seats for people who never arrive will stop offering journeys.

Flow 01

Before-Journey Confirmation Flow

This flow covers the reminder and confirmation sequence before the journey begins. It brings the trip back to everyone’s attention before the moment of departure.

01
24–30 hours before

Driver and passengers receive a notification asking them to confirm, cancel or flag an issue.

02
One hour before

A second prompt asks users to confirm readiness, flag if running late, or cancel before departure.

03
Shared status

The system updates the journey status for everyone, so nobody is left guessing whether the trip is still going ahead.

Design rationale

The repeated confirmations are not meant to feel bureaucratic. They are the app’s version of the informal messages people already send each other: “Are we still on for tomorrow?”, “Leaving soon”, and “Are you ready?” For strangers or loosely connected users, Tóggo standardises that reassurance.

Flow 02

Time-of-Departure Sequence Flow

This flow covers the live journey sequence. Once the trip begins, the system manages departure, pickup confirmation, passenger status, arrival and Pool Points.

01
Driver departs

The driver confirms departure and passengers receive a live journey status or ETA.

02
Passenger boards

Each passenger confirms they are in the car at the point of pickup, creating a reliable boarding record.

03
Journey completes

Arrival is confirmed and Pool Points are distributed to participants who completed the journey sequence.

Reliability and reward are the same gesture

Pool Points are not separate from the confirmation flow. They motivate users to show up, confirm, communicate and complete journeys honestly. Each confirmation becomes a small commitment that is immediately recognised by the system.

Testing Insight

Retained, but simplified

User testing found the confirmation system was generally well received. The average trust score was 3.9 out of 5 when participants read about the system, dropping slightly to 3.6 after completing the flow in the prototype. Five of seven participants found it reliable or acceptable, so the system was retained.

01

The post-confirmation screen was simplified because testers saw the intermediate live-status screen as unnecessary.

02

Push notification shortcuts could reduce friction, but most users did not know they existed, so they would need onboarding or a tooltip.

03

The final flow returns users to their previous context after confirmation instead of forcing them through an extra screen.

Outcome

Together, the two sequence flows show how a journey planned days or weeks in advance can arrive at departure time with each participant informed, confirmed and accountable — not through enforcement, but by making engagement the easiest and most rewarding path.

Mid-Fidelity & Testing

Testing Safety, Reliability, and Journey Organisation

Mid-fidelity testing focused on three areas that carried the most product risk: safety and verification, the relay confirmation system, and journey organisation. Each test compared a specific design decision, measured how users responded, and then informed what moved forward into the final prototype.

01

Safety

Tested how much verification users would accept, what could be deferred, and how unverified users should appear in the interface.

02

Reliability

Tested whether repeated confirmations increased trust or made the system feel excessive.

03

Organisation

Tested trip creation layouts, schedule views, quick actions, alerts and recurring journey controls.

01 / Safety

Verification needed to build trust without blocking entry.

Every user must be verified before connecting with others. The research showed that users wanted verified profiles, ratings and vetting, but testing also revealed friction around how much information users were willing to provide themselves.

3.9/5
Average comfort score

The 10-step sign-up flow was tolerated when framed as protection for other users rather than as a gate.

5/7
Rated 4 or 5

Most participants accepted the onboarding length when the safety value was clear.

1/7
Rated 2

One participant found the verification flow too heavy, showing that friction still needed to be reduced.

Safety decisions
  • Face verification was retained as non-negotiable.
  • Driver verification can be deferred, but driving remains locked.
  • Unverified profiles are greyed out in search results.
  • Vehicle images were reduced from three to two.
02 / Reliability

The confirmation system was kept, but simplified.

The confirmation system tests user reliability, not the platform’s. The app cannot make people reliable, but it can prompt, remind and reward the behaviours that make shared journeys work.

3.9/5
Before completing the flow

Users generally understood the value of repeated confirmation when it was explained as a reliability feature.

3.6/5
After completing the flow

Trust dropped slightly after use, suggesting the system worked but needed less friction.

1/7
Knew the notification shortcut

The hold-down notification action needs to be taught through onboarding or a first-time tooltip.

Reliability decisions
  • The three-stage confirmation system was retained.
  • The intermediate live journey screen was removed.
  • Confirmation now resolves directly after the action.
  • Notification shortcuts require explicit teaching.
03 / Organisation

Journey organisation needed clearer editing, scheduling and alerts.

Organisation testing looked at how users plan trips, manage recurring schedules, understand who is driving, and take quick actions without disrupting the whole group.

03A
Trip creation layout

A card carousel was tested against a single-page accordion. The accordion was preferred because each confirmed section could be edited directly.

85.7%
Accordion

The edit path was clearer because each confirmed section had its own edit control.

14.3%
Carousel

The carousel was more compact, but its edit path was less obvious.

03B
Recurring trip schedule

Calendar and roster layouts were tested. Calendar was selected because recurring trips are naturally understood in weekly and monthly blocks, while roster information was still useful for day-to-day detail. The final direction incorporated roster information into the calendar using colour coding, with separate outbound and return strips to show who is driving on each day.

42.9%
Calendar

Best for planning recurring trips and understanding weekly or monthly travel patterns.

28.6%
Roster

Useful for seeing trip-by-trip responsibility, but weaker for viewing the full driver rotation.

28.6%
Both

Suggested that the final design needed both a broad calendar overview and clearer day-level roster detail.

Prototype Evidence
How the schedule view was resolved

The final schedule direction combined the strengths of both tested options. Monthly view gives users a broad overview of recurring journeys, while weekly view gives clearer day-by-day detail for planning, checking roles, and managing changes.

Organisation decisions
  • Accordion trip creation was confirmed.
  • Calendar with monthly and weekly modes was retained.
  • Running days were renamed to recurring days.
  • Roster information was incorporated into the calendar using colour-coded journey states.
  • Monthly and weekly schedule views were kept to support both overview and day-level planning.
  • Drag-to-select was noted as a future development specification.
Testing outcome

The mid-fidelity tests clarified which interaction patterns should move into high fidelity. Verification remained central, confirmation was simplified, trip creation moved to an accordion model, and recurring journeys kept a calendar-led structure with both monthly and weekly views.

Final Artefact

Final App Prototype Flows

The final prototype demonstrates the key interaction flows that define Tóggo: onboarding, posting a journey, confirming reliability, authenticating a live journey, managing recurring plans, and accepting a match.

Flow 01

Onboarding and Authentication

This flow introduces the user to Tóggo, explains why verification is required, and frames authentication as a trust-building step rather than a generic account setup task.

The aim is to make users understand that identity checks protect the whole carpooling network before they begin posting or joining journeys.

Flow 02

Post a Trip

The post-a-trip flow shows how users create a journey by setting their role, route, date and time, seats, pricing model, pickup rules, and access settings.

The accordion structure keeps each section editable and makes the journey setup feel controlled, especially where different trip types produce different outcomes.

Flow 03

Relay Confirmations and Action Options

The relay confirmation flow demonstrates the reminder system that brings a planned journey back to users’ attention before departure. Users can confirm, cancel, flag an issue, or update their status.

This flow standardises the informal “are we still on?” messages that usually happen between people, while using Pool Points to reward users for staying engaged.

Flow 04

Authenticate Journey: Face ID

This flow shows how a user confirms their presence at a live journey stage using Face ID. Authentication helps verify that the person joining or continuing the trip is the verified account holder.

This supports journey accountability and helps protect the integrity of Pool Points by linking confirmation to the actual verified participant.

Flow 05

Authenticate Journey: Biometric Check

This flow demonstrates biometric journey authentication, such as a fingerprint or device-level biometric check, depending on the user’s device settings.

The interaction is designed as a quick confirmation step during the live journey, helping the system verify participation without adding unnecessary friction.

Flow 06

Schedule Planner Run-Through

The schedule planner flow shows how recurring journeys are managed across weekly and monthly views. Users can review recurring days, departure times, driver rotation, alerts, and participation states.

This flow was designed to make recurring carpooling feel organised and predictable, while still allowing users to respond to changes in the schedule.

Flow 07

View and Accept a Match

The match request flow shows how users review a potential journey match, understand the route, timing, participant role, verification status, and journey conditions before accepting.

The goal is to make matching feel informed and accountable, rather than anonymous, vague, or uncertain.

Outcome

What the Project Demonstrated

Tóggo demonstrates how interaction design can address the human side of a transport problem while also acknowledging that wider legal and policy support would be needed for full implementation.

Human Problem

The app addresses trust, safety, reliability, commitment, and journey organisation — the everyday issues that decide whether someone would actually share a car journey.

Interaction Response

The prototype translates research into practical interaction patterns: verification, schedule planning, relay confirmation, journey rules, and visible accountability.

Policy Boundary

The project also shows where design cannot act alone. Insurance, cost-sharing, tax treatment, SPSV boundaries, and public incentives need clearer policy support.
